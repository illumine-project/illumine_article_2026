\subsection{Compression}
\textit{Strawman owners: Cappello and Underwood $\sim$1200 words. for this subsection}

As Beam line facilities at the US DOE labs deploy larger and faster detectors to enable new scientific discovery, the volume of data becomes a more and more pressing infrastructure challenge to move from the edge near the beamline to the leadership class computing facilities where the data is ultimately analyzed and stored.
These trends are not isolated to any particular light source.
To illustrate, At the Advanced Photon Source, an X-ray Photon Correlation Spectroscopy (XPCS) beamline is expected to generate upto 168GB/s \cite{nicholasschwarzetalAPSScientificComputing2024} -- more than three times the bandwidth supported by the link from the APS to the Argonne Leadership Computing Leadership Facility shared across all beamlines.
At Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS), Serial Femptosecond Crystallography (SFX) beamlines expect to generate on the order of 1TB/s after the LCLS-II-HE upgrade is complete \cite{underwoodROIBINSZFastSciencePreserving2023} which dwarfs by a factor of 375 times the 2.8GB/s currently achievable between the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC) and LCLS \cite{blaschkeRealtimeXFELData2024} when using a reserved Energy Science Network (ESNet) circuit.
This trend drives facilities to explore data reduction techniques including compression to manage the data volume produced by these instruments as they concurrently pursue infrastructure updates to enable real or near-real time data analysis for light source experiments using leadership class computing needed for applications such as experiment steering.

However, existing general purpose compression techniques cannot meet the demands of these pressing challenges.
General purpose lossless compression techniques struggle to achieve compression ratios only 2 to 5.7 \cite{huangLsCOMPEfficientLight2025} for techniques such as XPCS at data rates far below line rate.
In contrast, specialized lossy compression techniques developed in the course of this project can achieve compression ratios near 40 without a meaningful impact on downstream analysis such as full width at half max speckle analysis \cite{huangLsCOMPEfficientLight2025} while meeting the substantial data rates expected for future experiments \cite{nicholasschwarzetalAPSScientificComputing2024}.
However, to safely deploy lossy compression techniques which selectively remove insignificant precision to achieve larger compression ratios, it is critical to co-design the compressor with the application to ensure scientific fidelity is presserved while meeting production demands.

One such major outcome is lsCOMP \cite{huangLsCOMPEfficientLight2025}, a GPU-based compression framework specifically designed for the irregular unsigned integer data generated by APS applications. 
Unlike conventional compressors that are often designed for general-purpose byte streams or smooth scientific data, lsCOMP supports configurable lossless and lossy compression through adaptive scalar quantization, selective pooling, and zero-bit elimination. 
These operations are integrated into a highly optimized single-GPU-kernel design, allowing lsCOMP to achieve compression throughput of hundreds of GB/s on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU while maintaining high compression ratios and application-level data quality. 
The resulting tool has been open-sourced \url{https://github.com/szcompressor/lsCOMP}.
Beyond GPU compression, we have explored how compression can be integrated more deeply into the APS end-to-end light-source data path. 
In particular, light-source workflows involve heterogeneous instruments, computing platforms, networks, and storage systems with substantially different resource and bandwidth constraints. 
This makes compression a system-level optimization problem rather than simply a standalone compression-kernel problem: an approach that performs well at one stage may not provide the best overall performance when data must traverse the complete workflow.
We expect to continue this work in the years to come as we expand our efforts both to additional beamlines and to additional facilities within the project.


We have also explored learning-assisted compression to exploit redundancy that is difficult for conventional numerical compressors to capture. 
Light source datasets can exhibit complex correlations across measurements, spatial regions, and time, creating opportunities for learned prediction to reduce the amount of information that must be explicitly encoded. 
However, these correlations vary considerably across experiments and scientific applications, making it challenging, even with Transformer models, to achieve high prediction accuracy, user-acceptable inference overhead, and reliable reconstruction simultaneously. 
Together, these explorations highlight the need to co-design compression algorithms with the characteristics of light source data and the systems through which those data move and are stored.
